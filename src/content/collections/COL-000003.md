## Overview

The development of networked cameras introduced a new form of digital
presence: the ability to observe distant subjects through images delivered
over computer networks.

Initially employed for practical purposes within technical environments,
networked imaging subsequently expanded into the observation of public
places and the deliberate publication of personal everyday life.

This collection brings together the Trojan Room Coffee Pot, FogCam, and
JenniCam. Together, these Digital Objects illustrate different stages in
the development of networked visual observation during the formative years
of the public Internet and World Wide Web.

## From Utility to Presence

The Trojan Room Coffee Pot originated as a practical solution to a mundane
problem. Researchers at the University of Cambridge used a networked camera
to determine remotely whether coffee was available before travelling to the
room containing the machine.

When the image service became accessible through the World Wide Web, an
internal technical convenience became an internationally accessible
networked view of a physical object.

FogCam extended networked imaging into persistent observation of place.
Established at San Francisco State University in 1994, the service
periodically published views from the university campus and continued
operating across changing locations, equipment, and custodians.

JenniCam applied similar technologies to a fundamentally different subject.
Beginning in 1996, Jennifer Ringley used an automated webcam system to
publish recurring images from her living spaces. The project transformed
networked imaging into a form of personal publication in which ordinary
daily life itself became the subject of continuing online observation.

Together, the three objects demonstrate a progression from observing an
object, to observing a place, to observing a person's everyday life.

## Preservation Paths

The objects also illustrate different outcomes in the preservation of
historical networked services.

The original Trojan Room Coffee Pot service ceased operation in 2001 and
is represented principally through surviving documentation, images,
archived material, and associated artifacts.

FogCam remains operational. Following a proposed closure in 2019,
responsibility for the service was transferred to San Francisco State
University, allowing the historical Digital Object to continue under
institutional stewardship.

JenniCam ceased operation in 2003. Its continuously generated imagery was
not preserved as a single complete corpus, leaving a fragmentary historical
record distributed across web archives, surviving images, contemporary
reporting, and other documentation.

These different preservation states demonstrate that historically related
Digital Objects may survive through documentation, continued operation, or
fragmentary archival evidence.

## Curatorial Note

Networked Eyes does not treat all networked cameras as historically or
socially equivalent.

The subjects, purposes, audiences, and expectations surrounding the three
systems differed substantially. A laboratory coffee machine, a university
campus, and an individual's living space present fundamentally different
questions of observation, participation, privacy, and public access.

The collection instead examines the technological and cultural expansion
of networked visual observation during the 1990s and early 2000s.

Particular care is required when interpreting JenniCam. Historical public
availability of material does not create an automatic presumption that all
surviving imagery should be republished. The Bureau distinguishes the
preservation and description of a historically significant Digital Object
from unrestricted reproduction of all material generated through it.
